GMST rotates TEME to the Earth-fixed PEF approximation; UT1 is approximated by UTC and polar motion omitted. The camera and geographic grid use a WGS84 ellipsoid and a declared geodetic observer (Sydney at 58 m by default). The orbital view is Earth-fixed; Earth does not rotate beneath an inertial camera in this edition.

JavaScript truncates the source epoch's sub-millisecond fraction; the full source epoch remains inspectable. There is no state interpolation. The **previous 60 study seconds** form a 31-point model trail sampled every two seconds, rebuilt directly and identically on seek or reverse. The [numerical audit](docs/evidence/iss-numerical-2026-10-08.json) measures a maximum roughly **4 m** chord error on five-minute-spaced checks throughout the study, below a 6 m test ceiling. This is trail approximation error, not overall orbital prediction accuracy. Fixed elements do not model unrecorded manoeuvres; the 24-hour eligibility rule is editorial rather than an accuracy guarantee.

Independent **Python sgp4 2.27 / Vallado C++** references cover ISS, low-orbit and deep-space verification cases at positive and negative epoch offsets, including a decayed case. TEME tests use a 2 cm position tolerance and 0.1 mm/s velocity tolerance. Astropy **8.0.1 / PyERFA 2.0.1.5** independently checks WGS84 observer geometry, Earth-fixed positions and above/below-horizon crossings. Its IERS polar motion explains part of the allowed 50 m Earth-fixed and 0.01° horizon differences. Independent pressure-zero Sun geometry also agrees within 0.02° at the selected pass and study bounds. Reference tolerances validate implementation agreement, not source accuracy.

The geometric pass overlays an idealised stellar sky. In the surface view, an authored solar-altitude fade is fully bright below −18° and disappears by −6°; daytime stars are suppressed. This is a compositional twilight rule rather than atmospheric photometry. Satellite glyphs and trails use authored screen size and brightness. A finite solar disc and spherical Earth of radius 6,378.137 km classify movers as sunlit, in partial shadow or in umbra at the displayed packet UTC. An eased display weight dims glyphs and historical trail samples to half strength in umbra; illumination never removes an object. Attachments inherit their parent geometry, and the selected record reports modelled illumination. Historical samples use their own UTC. This shadow model does not predict optical visibility: satellite reflectance, attitude, atmospheric extinction and actual observing conditions are not reconstructed. Stellar proper motion, annual aberration, parallax and refraction remain omitted. B−V uses an authored palette, missing colour is neutral, and variable-star brightness remains static.

**How a debris cloud forms** is an explicitly **indicative simulation**, available in Discover at `./how-a-debris-cloud-forms/`. Two synthetic bodies, with event-informed 72.5° encounter latitude and 86.4°/74° orbital inclinations, meet on idealised circular paths at 800 km and release 384 representative samples each. The clock now covers **500 original orbits, about 35 days**. Guided playback slows continuously through contact, with no fixed-time hold, follows the young cloud, then advances through 3, 25, 100, 250 and 500 revolutions. Native rates include **0.1, 1, 5 and 20 orbits/s**, alongside the close-view rates. The scrubber follows the selected viewing sequence while the elapsed readout reports physical model time. Playback stops at the bound; reduced motion opens/replays paused and never automatically restarts.

The seeded 50–1,500 m/s maximum release range produces different orbital periods, perigees, apogees and inclinations. Each equal-weight family's mean retains 97% of its incoming velocity relative to the shared centre of mass, keeping the prescribed primary release dissipative over the slider range. Optional first-order **J₂ secular precession** turns orbital planes and apsides at their own rates; the release elements are treated as mean elements, with short-period corrections omitted. Direct seeks do not integrate prior playback history. A sample is removed at its first radial crossing of a prescribed spherical 120 km entry boundary. Drag above that boundary is omitted, so this is a gravity/divergence experiment rather than a lifetime model. **Orbits & model** links a Gabbard-style perigee/apogee–period plot to the scene; future samples hide and removed ones dim. A primary NASA report supplies context for the real 2009 orbital distributions without importing or inventing historical element sets.

**Secondary** enables a controlled encounter at orbit 100. A surviving primary fragment is assigned a 1 kg mass; a synthetic 1,000 kg target is placed on a circular orbit intersecting it. The actual relative velocity and impact energy per gram are calculated. Both participants are consumed at contact; 256 new pink samples are released about the combined centre-of-mass velocity with paired kicks bounded by available relative kinetic energy. They follow their own J₂-perturbed orbits, with the same entry boundary. The encounter time is imposed, not a collision-probability result; this demonstrates how another breakup adds projectiles without claiming a population-wide cascade forecast. Secondary mode has its own slowed impact and cloud-follow passage before returning to the 500-orbit clock. Disabling it restores the primary-only counterfactual.

The viewing clock uses monotone cubic pacing with continuous, positive speed through both impacts. Materials and buffers are warmed before playback so the first debris frame does not compile shaders. Body diagrams, fragment shapes, display sizes and tumbling are authored. A floating origin preserves local precision; the target-plane scale bar and model-relative short histories make the close view legible. Fragment centres use one linear distance scale. The synthetic module remains separate from all GP loaders and catalogued identities and makes no provider requests. The [model audit](docs/evidence/collision-experiment-2026-11-01.json) pins implementation, assumptions, references and numerical checks, including independent Cartesian J₂ integration of nodal drift and secondary-release momentum/energy checks. Existing geography and catalogue releases remain unchanged.

The canonical brief also leaves separate optional chapters: a dense constellation with its own source and performance budget; historical accumulation with historical membership and elements; historical debris evolution with event-specific evidence; and optical visibility modelling or authored sound. Gaia stellar depth and much longer clocks remain distant optional research. These investigations are not prerequisites for the current edition.
