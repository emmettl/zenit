From an encounter to a field.

Five hundred revolutions

The clock covers about 35 days. Release velocities change orbital size, shape and inclination immediately. Different orbital periods spread the fragments around Earth; differential J₂ precession then separates their orbital orientations.

The original circular orbit takes . Playback includes 0.1, 1, 5 and 20 original orbits per second. Guided playback slows into contact, follows the young cloud, then advances through days.

Why the 2009 cloud is wider

The real Iridium 33–Cosmos 2251 breakup produced a broad range of perigees, apogees and orbital periods. NASA’s published Gabbard diagrams show this range. Later evolution also includes atmospheric drag and other perturbations. This experiment demonstrates release and differential precession using synthetic initial conditions; it does not fit the historical fragment population.

One fragment can make another cloud

Use Secondary in the timeline to enable or remove the controlled encounter. A surviving representative fragment is assigned a 1 kg mass; a 1,000 kg synthetic body is placed on an intersecting orbit. At contact both are consumed, and 256 new, equally weighted samples share their combined momentum. Paired release kicks use less than the available relative kinetic energy. Pink marks this new family.

Impact energy is expressed per gram of target mass. The frequently used catastrophic-breakup convention is 40 J/g. The target and encounter time are set by this experiment; there is no calculated collision probability or claimed cascade timetable. A population-wide risk simulation also needs physical sizes, surrounding traffic, avoidance, lifetimes and repeated probabilistic encounters.

The release and propagation

Two synthetic, equal-weight bodies meet at 800 km on idealised circular paths, with relative speed. The 72.5° encounter latitude and 86.4°/74° inclinations are informed by NASA’s published 2009 analysis. The circular states and fragment release are synthetic; this is not a historical reconstruction. Each releases 384 representative samples. Paired, opposite kicks fill a ball in velocity space, up to the slider’s 50–1,500 m/s maximum. The seed, directions and relative magnitudes stay fixed on replay. Each family retains 97% of its incoming velocity relative to the shared centre of mass, keeping the prescribed primary release dissipative throughout the slider range.

Elliptic Kepler propagation is augmented with first-order secular J₂ rates for node, perigee and mean anomaly. Release elements are treated as mean elements; short-period gravity corrections are omitted. Samples are removed on their first model crossing of a spherical 120 km entry boundary. This is not a drag model: orbital decay above that boundary, solar activity, radiation pressure and higher gravity terms are omitted. The 35-day view explains gravitational divergence, not fragment lifetimes.

All distances use one linear scale, with an arbitrary Earth orientation at contact. Glyph shapes, sizes and rotation are authored. Fragment centres follow the model. Short local histories use a frame translating with the mean release orbit. The scale bar reports distance in the target plane. The scrubber uses guided pace; its value text and elapsed clock report physical model time.

The 768 primary samples and 256 conditional secondary samples are representative markers, not a predicted debris census. Release heights use a spherical 6,378.137 km reference; the drawn Earth is the edition’s WGS84 ellipsoid.
