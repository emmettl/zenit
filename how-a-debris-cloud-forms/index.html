AN AUTHORED EXPERIMENT

Gravity stretches the cloud.

A velocity change alters a fragment’s orbit and its orbital period. Even when fragments leave together, some soon run ahead of others.

Here, two synthetic, equal-weight bodies meet at 800 km on circular paths crossing at 90°. Their relative speed is . Neither body represents an actual satellite or historical event.

What the slider changes

At contact, each body becomes 96 equally weighted representative samples. Each receives a direction sampled uniformly over a sphere, paired with its opposite, and the selected speed in m/s. The seed and directions stay fixed on replay. Colour preserves the originating body.

Each family’s mean velocity retains 99.5% of its incoming velocity relative to the shared centre of mass. This assumed impulse conserves total linear momentum and reduces total kinetic energy at every slider setting. It is an illustration of release and orbital motion; it does not calculate material fracture or fragment yield.

Different periods, different paths

These are the minimum and maximum periods of the samples, recalculated when you change the release speed. One original circular orbit takes .

Motion is solved directly from each release state with elliptic Kepler propagation, over three original orbital periods. The view has fixed inertial axes; Earth rotates beneath it, with an arbitrary orientation at contact. Distances use one linear scale. Points and direction arrows have authored display sizes.

No drag, oblateness, secondary collisions, long-term decay, size distribution or collision probability is modelled. The 192 samples are a visual experiment, not a predicted debris count. Release height is measured above a spherical 6,378.137 km reference; the drawn Earth uses the edition’s WGS84 shape.
